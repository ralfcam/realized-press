Still-Face Disruption of Expected Social Contingency

When a familiar adult suddenly becomes unresponsive in face-to-face interaction, infants typically protest, look away, and try to re-engage; this is a laboratory signature of expected contingency, not a diagnosis of attachment or co-regulation failure.

Quantitative Anchors
Tronick still-face paradigm: 2 minutes normal play, 2 minutes maternal frozen face, 2 minutes reunioninfant positive affect drops by >60%, gaze aversion increases, and self-soothing behaviors elevate during still facereunion rebound effect: heightened negative affect persists into initial recovery phase before homeostatic regulation returns

What We Know (Narrowest Supported Conclusion)

In the still-face procedure, a familiar adult who has been interacting normally becomes suddenly unresponsive. Infants typically reduce smiling, increase negative affect and gaze aversion, and often try to re-engage the adult. Tronick, Als, Adamson, Wise & Brazelton (1978) established the laboratory signature. Mesman, van IJzendoorn & Bakermans-Kranenburg (2009) synthesized decades of replications: the protest/re-engagement pattern is robust as a procedure effect, not as a personality score.

Evidence and Causal Assessment

The design is experimental: the same adult, the same infant, a controlled change in contingency. That is stronger than a correlational “synchrony score.” It is still a laboratory episode. Mesman et al. (2009) document heterogeneity by age, coding, and recovery-period measurement. The existing co-regulation card covers bidirectional regulation as a construct; this card covers one experimental disruption of that process. Sleep and attachment-intervention cards are not restated.

Effect Size and Persistence

The classic result is a within-episode change in affect and looking, not a portable standardized “regulation trait.” Persistence as a forecast of later competence is not what the paradigm measures.

Bias, Heterogeneity, and Confounding

GI attenuation: n/a. Cultural display rules, infant state, and how “recovery” is coded all move the score. A difficult still-face is not institutional deprivation.

What This Does Not Mean (Strict Boundary Conditions)

Does not mean a distressed still-face proves insecure attachment or parental failure. Distress is information about broken contingency, not a purity test. Co-regulation is not constant calm.

What Remains Uncertain

How still-face recovery relates to everyday repair; whether procedure intensity predicts later anxiety once temperament is accounted for.

What Action Is Reasonably Justified

Ordinary, repairable interaction is consistent with the finding. Training parents to “pass” a still-face, or ranking infants by protest, does not follow.
