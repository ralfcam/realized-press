# Responsive Caregiving and Attachment Interventions

> Domain: responsive caregiving / attachment
> Last updated: 2026-09-14
> Status: Preliminary (initial literature pass; not yet deeply audited)

## What We Know

Randomized interventions that train parental sensitivity produce a medium-sized increase in sensitive caregiving and a smaller but real increase in infant attachment security; short, focused, behaviorally-oriented interventions outperform longer or more diffuse ones.

## How Confident We Are

**Grade**: Moderate-to-High for the sensitivity-intervention effect; Moderate for the downstream attachment-security effect, given consistent meta-analytic replication across multiple independent syntheses.

## Evidence and Causal Assessment

- Bakermans-Kranenburg, van IJzendoorn & Juffer (2003) meta-analysis: 70 studies, 88 intervention effects (sensitivity N=7,636; attachment N=1,503). Randomized interventions: d=0.33 for sensitivity, d=0.20 for attachment security.
- Earlier van IJzendoorn et al. (1995) meta-analysis (16 studies, N=869) found d=.58 for sensitivity change and d=.17 for attachment security change; short-term preventive interventions outperformed longer/more intensive/therapeutic approaches.
- De Wolff & van IJzendoorn (1997) meta-analysis of parental antecedents of attachment (66 studies, N=4,176) established sensitivity as a moderate, non-exclusive predictor of attachment security.

## Effect Size and Persistence

Sensitivity change: d~0.33-0.58 (medium). Attachment security change: d~0.17-0.20 (small). These are intervention (efficacy) effects from mostly short-term RCTs; long-run persistence into middle childhood and beyond is not established by this literature pass.

## Bias, Heterogeneity, and Confounding

GI attenuation: **n/a** (randomized sensitivity intervention trials, not an observational population phenotype).

Moderator analyses consistently show shorter, more behaviorally focused interventions outperform longer/more clinical ones. Effectiveness in multi-problem/high-risk families appears comparable to lower-risk families, suggesting some scalability, but effectiveness trials and cross-cultural replication outside Western/high-income settings are less represented.

## What Remains Uncertain

Whether attachment-security changes translate into lasting differences in child socio-emotional outcomes; how intervention effects generalize to non-Western caregiving contexts; long-term institutional scalability.

## What This Does Not Mean

Does not establish that insensitive parenting is a dominant determinant of child outcomes, nor that all attachment insecurity is parent-caused.

## What Action Is Reasonably Justified

Short, focused, behaviorally-oriented sensitivity-training programs are a reasonably well-supported intervention target for services aiming to support caregiving quality, particularly in higher-risk families; this does not justify blanket judgments about individual parenting style.
